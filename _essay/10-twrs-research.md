Surrounded by wilderness, TWRS is ideally situated for wilderness research studies. Researchers from the University of Idaho, Idaho State University, The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, Idaho Fish and Game, NASA, and Boise State University conduct research at Taylor Ranch. The vast, everchanging landscape provides an array of topics to study [15].

## Mountain Lion & Big Game Range

The first research done at Taylor was in 1964 when Dr. Maurice Hornocker, at the time a graduate student, began studying mountain lions. This was the first major study ever done on the species. The research that Dr. Hornocker (pictured on the left) and his local hounds-man Wilbur Wiles (pictured on the right) conducted, became essential to changing the status of mountain lions in Idaho from bounty animal to a big game species. They continued the studies into the 70's where Dr. Hornocker then transitioned to advising graduate students who continued his research. Many academic articles have been published on the mountain lions and big game along the Big Creek area [8,16].

Dr. Hornocker went on to become a pioneer in big cat research, authoring and co-authoring more that 100 scientific publications. His research took him to places all over the world conducting surveys on cougars, leopards, bobcats, lynx, Siberian tigers, jaguars, ocelot tigers, black bears, wolverines, grizzlies, and badgers. He founded The Hornocker Wildlife Institute in 1985. The non-profit dedicated to wildlife research and education was headquartered at the University of Idaho until it merged with the Wildlife Conservation Society in 2002 and moved to Bozeman, MT [17].

## Forest Owl Habitat

In 1981, Gregory Hayward and Dr. E.O. (Oz) Garton initiated owl research in habitat surrounding TWRS. They discovered the first nesting record for the boreal owl in Idaho. Additional research suggested that this year-round resident of Idaho was a key indicator species for high elevation spruce-fir forests [18].

In research conducted from 1985-1995, Anthony Wright and Gregory Hayward examined range expansion of Barred Owls (see photo at right). At first, the owls were not heard when playback calls were employed. However, in 1985 the owl populations were detected in upland mature and old-growth mixed conifer forests. The change in forest cover due to fluctuations in climate contributed to the Barred Owl's movement deeper into the Rocky Mountains. Evidence from this study showed that central Idaho was prime breeding habitat [19].

## Wintering Passerines

Passerines, or birds that have feet that are adapted for perching, includes all songbirds, and half of all bird species. Susan Tank and Winifred Sidle conducted a study on the habitat relationships to examine bird species abundance, in correlation to vegetation structural attributes. Five different vegetation types were used as the sample locations. This research was conducted on wintering passerines along the Big Creek Drainage to discover what patterns within the habitats could be found and if they related to the structural characteristics of the vegetation. The study found that wintering passerines were not using habitats based upon the structural features of the surrounding vegetation. Instead, the need for food supplies during a time in which snow covered the ground and limited foraging seemed to be a more accurate influence of habitat selection [20].

## Behavior & Relationships of Bighorn Sheep

During the late 1980s scientists became alarmed at the die-off of bighorn sheep. Due to the lack of livestock and little disturbance by people near Taylor Ranch, herds found along Big Creek made them prime populations to study. In cooperation with Idaho Fish and Game, research was conducted on the bighorn sheep. Holly Akenson, then one of the caretakers of TWRS, also conducted her Master's Thesis on the *Spatial Relationship and Behavior of Bighorn Sheep Sharing a Winter Range with Mule Deer and Elk in Central Idaho*.

> *"Each one of my swings of the splitting maul would hit the big Douglas fir round with a resounding 'thunk!' After one of these whacks, I heard a distinctive echo. Or was it? ## Coexisting Carnivores

Due to its wilderness setting, mountain lions, bobcats, and coyotes are all found living in habitats with similar features and preying upon similar species. Gary Koehler and Dr. Maurice Hornocker found that bobcats and mountain lions were more commonly associated with areas where there was timber and rocky terrain which provided cover for stalking. Whereas they found that coyotes had a wide distribution and tended to be found in open areas and throughout a variety of forest types. When observing ungulate kills, it was found that neither of the felids visited the kills made by coyotes. However, bobcat kills were visited by coyotes and mountain lions, and mountain lion kills were visited by coyotes and bobcats. There was overlap in each of the prey's habitats which increased in overlap during the winter months and even led to a few bobcat and coyote kills by mountain lions when food resources were low. This research was conducted to better understand the community structure within a large group of predators [22].

## Archaeological Excavations

In the early 1980's, archaeologists from the United States Forest Service and University of Idaho conducted limited excavations and surveys along Big Creek. The objectives of the research were to postulate a difference between the winter and summer settlements of the tribes that once lived in the Big Creek drainage.

Dr. Frank Leonhardy, a professor of Anthropology at the University of Idaho, headed up the first serious attempts to conduct archaeological investigations. Both surface collection and subsurface excavations took place at various sites. It was found that the people who once lived in the area were year-round inhabitants. They would often move to different areas based upon the time of year and food resources available. Two basic housing structures were determined in the village sites. The summer homes were a "Navajo hogan-style" dwelling that was large enough to house a family. The dome shaped pit houses found along the banks of Big Creek were situated partially underground and covered with inter-woven Red Osier Dogwood branches covered with animal hides. These features provided extra insulation during winter months. Many different kinds of sites were found including: rockshelters, cairns, pits, lithic scatters, and hunting blinds concealed in talus slopes. These sites were found in meadows, cirque lakes, terraces, tributary canyons, river bars, hillsides, and xeric ridges proving that the inhabitants moved often [38, 37, 41].

Many lithic artifacts were uncovered at the sites. Some of the projectile points and river cobbles are not found in this area, proving that they had been transported over a distance to be used for hunting and processing food. Faunal remains indicated that bighorn sheep were the primary food source in winter. It is thought that a local herd of 125 sheep would support approximately 15-20 people for a winter. If the herd continued to be hunted for successive seasons, it began to deplete to a point where the sheep would no longer be able to support the people hunting them as a food source. The research proposed that the small human groups would prey on one sheep herd for a single season, and then move on to another sheep herd allowing for population density within the human and animal groups to be sustainable [38, 39].

## Human And Ecological Change In The Frank Church - River Of No Return Wilderness

It's hard to see how something has changed when you can't see what it looked like previously. This is equally the case with the Big Creek drainage in the Frank Church. This area has a history of human-environmental relationships that are visible, yet we don't always know the full story of what has taken place over the years. As part of her graduate research with the University of Idaho, Micaela Petrini conducted rephotography work along Big Creek. This entailed her finding historical pictures and hiking around the wilderness trying to find the exact location and vantage point that the original image was taken. She would then take a photograph and log the GPS location. This comparison invites the viewer to observe and assume what the changes may have been that occurred between the time the two images were taken. Various stakeholders were interviewed to assess how they valued the changes.

To bring all of this work together, composer and musician Ruby Fulton, from the University of Idaho Lionel Hampton School of Music, wrote the *Wilderness Suite*. Her music takes non-musical ideas and explores them through sound. The music is moving as she combines the notes with the interviews to create a feeling of shift and change in the human-environmental relationships.

## Monitoring of Chinook and Steelhead Population in Big Creek

Idaho Fish and Game (IDFG) operate a rotary screw trap on Big Creek. This trap allows fisheries biologists to monitor the incredible journey of the Chinook Salmon and steelhead trout. The anadramous fish are captured as juveniles and implanted with PIT tags (passive integrated transponder). The individuals are then released back into the water to continue their journey to the Pacific Ocean and later return to their original spawning grounds. Their location can then be tracked every time they cross a PIT antennae. While, the individuals cannot be tracked in the ocean, as soon as they cross an antennae on their way back to their spawning grounds, their location is collected allowing biologists to track the individuals once again [23, 24].

<!-- image objectid=twrs-169; file/link=1593632255460.jpeg; Figure 46. Implanting a PIT tag into a wild Chinook Salmon at the South Fork Salmon River rotary screw trap. Notice the PIT tag, the glass capsule, protruding from the needle. -->

> *"This can even allow a single fish to be monitored from the time it was tagged, across the hundreds of miles to the ocean, through each of the 8 dams on the Columbia and Snake Rivers, and back as an adult" - Josh Poole, Regional Fishery Biologist [24].*

IDFG has been collecting data from Taylor since 2007. They PIT tag around 6,000 fish annually during the migration of Chinook and steelhead in Big Creek. By tagging the fish, biologists are able to calculate the estimated fish populations. For the Chinook, they are even able to calculate the survival of the species to the Lower Granite Dam (Snake River) and through the hydrosystem. These rotary screw traps and PIT tags have provided data to help determine the best management practices for these essential fish which provide marine-derived nutrients to the Idaho ecosystem [23, 24].
