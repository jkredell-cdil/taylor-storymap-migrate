## Current Happenings

Although TWRS no longer sees homesteaders, prospectors, and big game hunters crossing over the Big Creek bridge to stay, it is still very active.

Every year it becomes a temporary home to researchers, interns, students, professors, and government employees all working together to learn more and protect the vast landscape around them.

## Semester in the Wild

The courses in this fall semester are immersed in the heart of the wilderness. Earning 17 credit hours, undergraduate students live, work, and learn at the most remote teaching and learning facility in the lower 48. Students will have courses in ecology, wilderness management, outdoor leadership, environmental history, and environmental writing all while living in the Idaho wilderness [29].

## McCall Outdoor Science School

Located in McCall, Idaho, MOSS is a beautiful 14 acre campus on the shores of Payette Lake and surrounded by Ponderosa State Park. Operated by the University of Idaho's College of Natural Resources, the program provides a one-year graduate degree. While obtaining a Master in Natural Resources with an emphasis on Environmental Education and Science Communication. Students learn professional, educational, communication, scientific, and leadership skills while attending this one of a kind program.

During the spring semester, graduate students get the opportunity to spend several days at the Taylor Wilderness Research Station. This is usually one of the biggest highlights of the year as the semester draws to a close. The students' time at TWRS merges the coursework they've been taking with a unforgettable wilderness experience. This leaves them with an unforgettable adventure that forges a connection between the people around them and the place in which they are learning [30].

## Haven For Students & Firefighters

While TWRS is for research and educational purposes only, there are times when it welcomes visitors. During wildfire season, the small wilderness oasis sometimes becomes home to fire crews that need a place to stay. While the occasional hiker may be given a meal and a grassy meadow to lay their head, travel to TWRS requires a reservation request that must be approved by the College of Natural Resources Administration.

## Current Indigenous Connections

Tribal use and interest of the wilderness continues. Many culturally significant sites are still present throughout the Frank Church River of No Return Wilderness. These American Indian sites are on the National Register of Historic Places and are protected through the Historic Preservation Plan (HPP). All organizations that are a part of the HPP, also work closely with the federally recognized Tribes to protect these culturally-sensitive locations [34].

The connection that the Tribal governments have with this landscape is ongoing. They continue to bring their people to these areas to show them the ways of their ancestors, share stories, and uphold traditions. The pictographs, home sites, and artifacts left behind by Tribal Ancestors are just a snapshot of what life was once like along Big Creek and in the surrounding mountains. As the Indigenous histories of this place continue, it builds a heritage that can continue to live on for generations.

For the Indigenous People, this place is not a River of No Return, but is a River of Return

## Leaving A Legacy

Much has changed at TWRS over the last 50+ years and throughout the history of the region. Yet, it still possesses an everlasting sense of beauty and wonder. Much has been learned about the plants and animals that exist in these mountain, waterways, and valleys. Furthermore, the effects of a changing climate and human presence has led to drastic changes that have altered the ecological processes. However, a vast amount of knowledge has been gained from research conducted along Big Creek that has let to positive change to counteract and slow the drastic changes. Researchers will continue to test, explore, experiment, and investigate the ecological significance around TWRS.

## Looking Into The Future...

Change can be hard, but is essential to the growth and advancement of both people and places. As we move into a season of change for Taylor Wilderness Research Station it is with excitement! Programs are being evaluated, facilities are being organized, and past and present research is being assessed to provide information and ideas for future research opportunities. It is important to be able to share the data, publications, historical documents, media, and research associated with TWRS. A database collection has been developed that will provide digital access to these valuable resources. The time and energy being put into the Taylor Wilderness Research Station is being done to continue to provide an invaluable location for researchers to explore environmental issues and to provide a wilderness experience for generations to come.
