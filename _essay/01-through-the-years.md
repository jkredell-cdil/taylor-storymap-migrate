## Deep In The Heart

The University of Idaho's Taylor Wilderness Research Station, embedded within the heart of the Frank Church-River of No Return Wilderness, predates the Frank Church-River of No Return designation. Established in 1969 - five years after the Wilderness Act was passed in Congress - Taylor Ranch was established as a unique wilderness research station. For 50 plus years its 65 acre-campus has supported research and education programming on the social and ecological processes in wilderness. Surrounded by 2.39 million acres of wilderness, Taylor Ranch provides outstanding access, facilities, and logistical support for research focused on ecological processes in a natural environment.

This story map was created to share what a wonderful and unique place Taylor Wilderness Research Station (TWRS) is. TWRS is a wonderfully unique place, in part, because of its remote location. Even though only a limited number of visitors come to TWRS, it holds a special place in the heart of each visitor.

To provide some perspective on TWRS’s history, geography, ecology, and research, these maps highlight important locations and landmarks. Many people have been key players in the forming of TWRS into what it is today. As you scroll down hopefully you will be transported to another time and place but will also see the complex ways in which many parts move together to form ideas around the concept of wilderness, specifically the Frank Church River of No Return Wilderness.

## Indigenous Peoples Connections to the Landscape

The Nez Perce and Shoshone-Bannock tribes consider the Big Creek Tributary as part of their ancestral homelands. Other tribes have overlapped them in this region, including the Shoshone-Paiute, Confederated Salish, and Kootenai. The tribal connections with Big Creek as well as the Middle Fork of the Salmon River, Main Salmon River, and the entire wilderness area have existed since time immemorial and are still in the making [34].

The *Nimiipuu* or Nez Perce people moved throughout present day Idaho, Oregon, Washington, Montana, and Wyoming. Their off-reservation inherent rights have been upheld and allow for tribal members to fish, hunt, gather, and graze livestock on open unclaimed lands outside their reservation. This has allowed them to preserve their *nimiipuu’neewit* (traditional Nez Perce life-ways) [35,36].

The Shoshone-Bannock were known for their hunting and gathering food to eat in the winter. Moving with the seasons, they spent warmer months at higher elevations and cooler months at lower elevations. This tribe is a banding together of the Dukudeka, or Sheepeater band, as well as other bands. As their name implies, they were known for hunting bighorn sheep. They did not let a single part of the animal go to waste. They would craft bows from the ram horns to use for hunting. These bows were the perfect size for hunting bison and were a highly sought-after commodity during trades. These lands still have a cultural and religious significance for the Shoshone-Bannock. Their relationship to *dammen baa* "our water" brings them back to share the histories and stories to their future generations [40, 37].

## Settler Histories

Between the 1860s and the early 1900s, European-American settlements began to appear in the Idaho mountains. Towns such as Florence, Thunder Mountain, and Warren were built quickly and filled with prospectors in search of gold. Settlers came from the West and moved eastward into parts of Idaho in search of mining opportunities and land for ranches. The wild areas of the Big Creek drainage changed as people built homes, roads, and bridges. They brought in livestock and began taming the wilderness. Prospecting gave way to outfitters providing wilderness experiences to people interested in hunting, fishing, and the recreational opportunities provided by rugged central Idaho mountains [1].

Dealing with dramatically changing weather conditions and the remote location of the Big Creek drainage was no small task for people who called it home. Many moved on after attempting to make a living, but right behind them someone else moved in and tried to do the same.

<!-- image objectid=twrs-006; file/link=1596497281443.jpeg; Figure 3. United States Forest Service, Payette National Forest -->



Although a private man, "Cougar" Dave was known throughout Idaho and even across the United States by the time of his death in 1936. Born in Yoncalla, OR sometime between 1844 and 1855 he moved to Idaho as a young man and never returned to his family's farm. He preferred life in the central Idaho mountains. In 1879 he signed on as a packer for the two-mule ammunition train in the Sheepeater Campaign, in which the Army stepped in to protect miners from supposed attacks and raids from local Indigenous Tribes. Receiving pressure from miners to protect them, the Army dispatched two forces into the mountains to subdue the Dukudeka (Sheepeaters) and take them to the Fort Hall reservation [2,3].

It was during this time that Dave Lewis first saw the land along Big Creek. He went on to do some prospecting and packing here and there, but eventually ended up settling along Big Creek in 1918 when he was in his early seventies. Although many tall tales are centered around Dave Lewis and his big game hunting expeditions which often centered around killing cougars and once involved him chasing down one of the last grizzlies in the area. Lewis preferred to live a simple life, keeping to himself with his dogs, sheep, and mountains filled with big game. At the time, no predator management practices were in place, many predatory animals were treated as a threat and extirpated. As his name assumes, Cougar Dave was known for killing cougars. It is said that Dave Lewis killed over 1,000 cougars in his lifetime. While living along Big Creek, Dave's fame spread as a big game outfitter for many wealthy and well-known men. "Uncle Dave" was highlighted in newspapers in the West from the *Sunday Oregonian* and *The Idaho Statesman* all the way to the *Nashville Banner* and the *New York Times*. Hunters traveled far and wide to hunt with the old Idaho legend who was now well into his 80's.

One of Dave's friends recommended he try to claim his land and so he began the difficult process in 1924. Unfortunately, he received a lot of government push back because he did not claim the land when he had initially moved to the area. However, after much negotiation and help from many of his friends and acquaintances, Dave received a signed patent to his place in February of 1928 from President Calvin Coolidge himself. Dave Lewis finally owned his property - the Lewis Place [2,3].

Establishing the Idaho Primitive Area started with a conversation around a campfire in the wild heartland of the Idaho mountains. During a hunting trip off of Big Creek, a main tributary of the Middle Fork of the Salmon River, on October 1927, a lumber executive, the Idaho governor, a mining executive, district forester, photographer, and old hunter sat there discussing whether the land around them should become a game preserve or a primitive area. This discussion sparked a dream that would eventually come to fruition. Wilderness preservation was a growing topic in state and country governments of Idaho, and in 1931, the Idaho Primitive Area was set aside as 1,087,744 acres of Idaho wilderness [4].

> *"To make it possible for people to detach themselves, at least temporarily, from the strains and turmoil of modern existence, and to revert to simple types of existence in conditions of relatively unmodified nature [and] to afford unique opportunities for physical, mental, and spiritual recreation and regeneration." - Idaho Primitive Area Report [5].*

Idaho Senator Frank Church was an instrumental figure in the preservation of the central Idaho wilderness. His political actions were essential in the passage of the Wild and Scenic Rivers Act of 1968, the creation of the Sawtooth National Recreation area in 1972, the creation of the Gospel Hump Wilderness (Endangered Wilderness Act of 1978), and the Central Idaho Wilderness Act of 1980, an act that created a wilderness of 2,366,757 acres, the largest contiguous federally managed wilderness outside of Alaska. As a tribute to his efforts, the Idaho Primitive Area was renamed Frank Church-River of No Return Wilderness in 1984. This naming of the wilderness honors two heritages. The first part of the name - Frank Church - is in honor of the congressman whose actions made it possible for other Idaho officials to pursue the preservation and protection of keeping natural systems intact. The second part of the name refers to the early days of river transportation, when the Main Salmon River was called, "The River of No Return". While boats could navigate down the river, the many rapids and fast flowing water prevented their return [6].

## Big Creek

<!-- The regional wilderness GeoJSON files contain Taylor-area features only; use the linked ArcGIS web map for the full national layers. -->

Big Creek, which flows through Taylor Ranch, is a major tributary to the Middle Fork of the Salmon river. Not only does it support a wide variety of flora and fauna in this vast wilderness, but it has also been a secluded thoroughfare for humans for centuries. Indigenous tribes used these lands for hunting and as the gold rush began, prospectors began traversing the steep hillsides in search of wealth. Although the area was still remote and far from true civilization, a few people found their home along this river drainage surrounded by steep mountainsides.
