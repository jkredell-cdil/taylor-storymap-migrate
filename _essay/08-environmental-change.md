Although Taylor Wilderness Research station is isolated, it is still impacted by the environmental changes that continue to take place. Some are easy to see and quantify such as the devastation left after a raging wildfire. While some changes, such as the reintroduction of wolves, take longer to see what kind of impact they will have upon the landscape.

Wildfires are seen as both a positive and negative occurrence depending on the situation and who you are talking to. Many of the plants and animals in Idaho need the effects of fire in their habitats. However, it can also leave severe devastation in it's wake. In a recent study performed along Big Creek, a University of Idaho Graduate Student, Micaela Petrini, got the opportunity to interview people who have ties to the area. She found a common theme of fire was centered around the conversations. Fire was seen to be "playing a natural role in the landscape" through a more "natural fire regime now, than it used to be." The increased snags and deadfall from the Diamond Complex fire in the year 2000 has caused many trails to be unusable and the USDA Forest Service can't keep up with all the clearing that needs to be done. There is also increased erosion and more wood ending up in the rivers from the deadfall which effects the sediments in Big Creek. Fire is a natural process of the Idaho landscape, yet fire behavior has been influenced by land management practices and changes in climate. Wildfires are having more severe impacts due to the accumulation of flammable vegetation and litter build up from years of fire suppression.

## Gone But Not Forgotten

With the Idaho Primitive Area becoming a true designated wilderness area, this meant that the settlers had to move off any governmental land where they had homesteaded. Although they moved out, many structural remains along with old machinery were left deep in the mountains. Although some structures were dismantled and machinery was removed or buried, wildfires have played a big role in destroying these previous home sites causing there to be a reclamation of the landscape as it returns back to a natural setting.

## Change in Climate

With the climate shifting, the natural ecosystems begin to also shift. As temperature increases, there is less snowpack in the mountains, more rain events in winter, and increasing snow melt. The alteration in the snowpack influences stream discharge, erosion, the plants and animals that rely on that water, and it causes drier terrestrial conditions which can lead to a high wildfire potential. All these changes affect ecological processes that lead to species (animals and plants) adaptation. Tracking climatic conditions provides data to plan for projected changes and impact on wilderness. [13,14].

## Tracking Temperatures

The Taylor Wilderness Research Station has been tracking weather data since 1974. This includes temperatures, precipitation, and snowfall/snowdepth. This data has been collected through a variety of different methods during that time. However, 2020 marked a new year for TWRS and their weather tracking technology which will now include a weather station that will automatically track the different weather components. Allowing TWRS to continue tracking the climate for decades to come.
