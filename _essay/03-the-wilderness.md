Establishing the Idaho Primitive Area started with a conversation around a campfire in the wild heartland of the Idaho mountains. During a hunting trip off of Big Creek, a main tributary of the Middle Fork of the Salmon River, on October 1927, a lumber executive, the Idaho governor, a mining executive, district forester, photographer, and old hunter sat there discussing whether the land around them should become a game preserve or a primitive area. This discussion sparked a dream that would eventually come to fruition. Wilderness preservation was a growing topic in state and country governments of Idaho, and in 1931, the Idaho Primitive Area was set aside as 1,087,744 acres of Idaho wilderness [4].

> *"To make it possible for people to detach themselves, at least temporarily, from the strains and turmoil of modern existence, and to revert to simple types of existence in conditions of relatively unmodified nature [and] to afford unique opportunities for physical, mental, and spiritual recreation and regeneration." - Idaho Primitive Area Report [5].*

Idaho Senator Frank Church was an instrumental figure in the preservation of the central Idaho wilderness. His political actions were essential in the passage of the Wild and Scenic Rivers Act of 1968, the creation of the Sawtooth National Recreation area in 1972, the creation of the Gospel Hump Wilderness (Endangered Wilderness Act of 1978), and the Central Idaho Wilderness Act of 1980, an act that created a wilderness of 2,366,757 acres, the largest contiguous federally managed wilderness outside of Alaska. As a tribute to his efforts, the Idaho Primitive Area was renamed Frank Church-River of No Return Wilderness in 1984. This naming of the wilderness honors two heritages. The first part of the name - Frank Church - is in honor of the congressman whose actions made it possible for other Idaho officials to pursue the preservation and protection of keeping natural systems intact. The second part of the name refers to the early days of river transportation, when the Main Salmon River was called, "The River of No Return". While boats could navigate down the river, the many rapids and fast flowing water prevented their return [6].

## Big Creek

<!-- The regional wilderness GeoJSON files contain Taylor-area features only; use the linked ArcGIS web map for the full national layers. -->

Big Creek, which flows through Taylor Ranch, is a major tributary to the Middle Fork of the Salmon river. Not only does it support a wide variety of flora and fauna in this vast wilderness, but it has also been a secluded thoroughfare for humans for centuries. Indigenous tribes used these lands for hunting and as the gold rush began, prospectors began traversing the steep hillsides in search of wealth. Although the area was still remote and far from true civilization, a few people found their home along this river drainage surrounded by steep mountainsides.
